No. 1 · 25 Nov 2026
Postpartum mental disorder in Kazakhstan, 2014–2024 Republican Center for Electronic Healthcare · Bureau of National Statistics

The condition Kazakhstan's health statistics cannot see

Between January and October 2025, one inpatient psychiatric facility in Almaty treated eighty women for mental disorders arising in the postpartum period. The number recorded under F53, the diagnostic category for exactly that, was zero. Across the whole country and the whole decade, 271 women received a first-ever F53 diagnosis against 4,419,907 births — a rate roughly three orders of magnitude below what international prevalence estimates predict. This is a report on what that absence is made of, and on what artificial intelligence could and could not do about it.

Section 01 · The Problem

These women were already in hospital. The system simply did not write it down.

Source Khamidullina et al. (2025), J Clin Med 14(7):2418.

Postpartum depression begins within twelve months of giving birth — persistent sadness, anxiety, fatigue, difficulty sustaining daily function. It affects an estimated 10–20% of postpartum women worldwide. Most recover within a few months; around 30% go on to develop major depression beyond the first year.

Source Marat et al. (2026), Reproductive Medicine (Central Asia) 2:776, Tables 2 and 3.

Between 2014 and 2024, Kazakhstan’s official statistics recorded twenty-two episodes of treatment for postpartum mental disorder in Almaty — a city of more than two million people. Across those eleven years, not one woman in the city was entered on the dispensary register for the condition.

Source Marat et al. (2026), Table 5. Inpatient records, one Almaty institution, Jan–Oct 2025.

Between January and October 2025, one inpatient psychiatric facility in Almaty treated eighty women for mental disorders arising in the postpartum period. The diagnoses recorded were anxiety and mixed anxiety-depressive (21.3%), schizophrenia (17.5%), depressive episode (16.3%), stress and adjustment (12.5%), acute psychotic (11.3%), and others. The number coded F53 — the ICD-10 category for exactly this — was zero.

Each stage follows from the one before it, and the last returns to the first. A condition the state cannot count is one it cannot plan for; a condition it does not plan for generates no data.

Data Bureau of National Statistics — live births by region and year. Registration figures: Marat et al. (2026), Table 1.

Kazakhstan registered 4,419,907 births between 2014 and 2024. Against that, 271 women in the entire country received a first-ever F53 diagnosis — between ten and forty a year. The highest any region reached in eleven years was 0.66 per 1,000 births, in Aktobe in 2018. International estimates put postpartum depression at 14–17%.

Over the same period the three registration series diverge. Incident diagnoses show no statistically significant trend (β = 0.027, p = 0.978), while the number of women under dispensary observation declines from 84 to 52 (β = −3.30, p = 0.001) and episodes of treatment fall from a peak of 49 in 2017 to 27 in 2024 (p = 0.002). Live births over the same eleven years decline by approximately eight per cent. Series that measure a single underlying condition would be expected to move together; these do not, which indicates that what is being recorded is a function of administrative practice rather than of incidence.

The reason is structural. F53 is a residual category — “not elsewhere classified” — reserved for cases that don’t fit an established diagnosis. A woman with depressive symptoms gets F32; a woman with psychosis gets F23 or F20. The coding in these cases conforms to the classification rather than departing from it.

ICD-10 does anticipate the difficulty. Code the disorder first, then add a second code from the obstetric chapter recording that onset fell within the puerperium. The association is preserved only where that second code is applied at the point of care and retained when the data is extracted. Kazakhstan's reporting system provides no field in which to record it, and the national series is therefore compiled from F53 alone. Marat and colleagues state the consequence directly: a statistical query based on F53 cannot reflect true prevalence.

Source Marat et al. (2026), Table 4 — treatment outcomes by year.

The cost falls first on women, invisibly. But the institutional losers are worth naming: health planners cannot allocate against a burden of 271 cases in a decade, and clinicians have no protocol to follow, because protocols are written for problems that appear in the statistics. The system itself registers no loss at all. It records improvement in 82 to 100 per cent of cases every year, and has recorded no recovery whatsoever since 2019.

Section 02 · Background & Data

Existing, Obtainable, and, Unfortunately, Empty Data

Measurements

Four bodies hold the relevant data. The Republican Center for Electronic Healthcare maintains F53 registration for every region of Kazakhstan: new diagnoses, dispensary follow-up, treated cases, treatment outcomes, and the coding practices underneath them. Births by region and year come from the Bureau of National Statistics. Workforce and facility counts come from the Ministry of Health’s annual statistical collections. The WHO Mental Health Atlas provides international comparison.

Marat and colleagues obtained the full registration series from the RCEH on official request, covering every region for 2014 to 2024. The data was never restricted: it was collected, held and maintained by the state throughout, and released when it was asked for. What the records fail to capture is not the data itself but the fact that a patient had recently given birth, because the reporting form contains no field in which to record it.

A newly diagnosed case is the first time a woman receives an F53 diagnosis. The dispensary figure counts women under observation in a given year, so a woman diagnosed in 2017 and still registered in 2019 enters the eleven-year total three times. Treated cases record episodes, not individuals. Of the three, only incident diagnoses approximate how often the condition is newly identified — and it is on that series that the comparison below rests, since a prevalence estimate predicts the appearance of new cases, not the accumulation of administrative records.

Section 03 · Current State of Research

How do we find it?

Prevalence and measurement

Sources Liu et al. (2022) J Clin Nurs 31:2665 · Wang et al. (2021) Transl Psychiatry 11:543 · Sun et al. (2023) Front Psychiatry 14:1273647 · Abenova et al. (2022) · Marat et al. (2025)

The prevalence literature is large and doesn’t agree. Pooled international estimates run from 14.0% (Liu et al. 2022) to 17.22% (Wang et al. 2021) to 20.42% (Sun et al. 2023). Kazakhstani figures sit above that band: 28.0% from Marat et al. (2025), using the first Kazakh-validated EPDS, and 59.4% from Abenova et al. (2022), screening-positive in Semey. The spread between these figures is not a function of where the studies were conducted but of how they were conducted, since every estimate is the product of a particular instrument applied at a particular threshold to a particular sample, and the resulting range covers a factor of four.

Health systems and task-sharing

Sources Rahman et al. (2008) Lancet · Nature Medicine (2025) NCT05353491 · Brown & Sprague (2021) BMC Public Health 21:1905

A second literature asks how the women who have it are treated. Rahman et al. (2008) showed in a cluster-randomised trial in rural Pakistan that lay health workers can deliver cognitive-behavioural techniques for perinatal depression without specialist supervision; the Thinking Healthy Programme became the WHO’s reference model for task-sharing in low-resource settings. A technology-assisted version was tested across seventy village clusters in 2025. Brown and Sprague (2021) sort the obstacles to care into four levels, from national policy down to the consulting room. A recent global review estimates that one woman in ten with diagnosed perinatal depression receives treatment in low- and middle-income countries. These interventions work. Each of them recruits women who have already been identified.

Figure 6 · Treatment reach in low- and middle-income countries

Of every ten women with a diagnosed perinatal depression in low- and middle-income countries, one receives treatment. The nine unfilled marks are women the system has already found. Everything in this cluster of the literature begins to the right of that point — after diagnosis, and after a service exists to refer into. Global review of perinatal depression, cited in the task-sharing literature. Figure shows a reported proportion, not a count.

Digital and AI intervention

Sources Li et al. (2023) npj Digit Med 6:236 · conversational agent meta-analysis, PMID 37115595 · Woebot Health WB001

A third literature asks whether technology can scale that treatment. Li et al. (2023), pooling fifteen randomised trials, find moderate effects for AI-based conversational agents on depression and on distress. The same analysis finds no significant effect on overall well-being: the estimate is 0.32, and its confidence interval crosses zero. A separate meta-analysis of conversational agent interventions finds that long-term effects across outcomes are not statistically significant at all. The most prominent regulated product in the field, Woebot’s WB001, received FDA breakthrough device designation for postpartum depression in 2021, entered a pivotal trial, and has since been discontinued. The evidence supports short-term symptom reduction. It does not establish durable gain, and it does not reach well-being. This literature presupposes what the previous one presupposed: a case already identified.

Administrative data and natural language processing

Sources Milan / ARS Toscana (2026) doi:10.1007/s00737-026-01766-3 · Ayre et al. (2021) doi:10.1371/journal.pone.0253809 · CRIS NLP Applications Library · HES diagnostic validity study

A fourth literature addresses identification itself, and it divides into two approaches. The first is record linkage. The Milan group’s analysis of administrative healthcare data (2026) follows 164,767 mothers who gave birth between 2017 and 2023, joining separate administrative databases to recover postpartum psychiatric morbidity that no single source holds on its own. The second is text processing. Ayre et al. (2021) apply natural language processing to the free-text clinical records held in the CRIS system at South London and Maudsley, evaluating the classifier against a manually coded reference standard rather than against the diagnostic codes — because the codes are the thing under examination. The CRIS library now lists more than eighty such applications in routine deployment. Separate validation work, comparing English Hospital Episode Statistics against CRIS records, shows that administrative psychiatric coding requires checking even in well-resourced systems. Both approaches recover un-coded cases at population scale. Both require that the infrastructure, and some record of the puerperal association, already exist.

Classification and legibility

Sources Bowker & Star (1999) Sorting Things Out, MIT Press · Scott (1998) Seeing Like a State · post-Soviet health systems scoping review, Heliyon (2024)

A fifth literature explains why that record may not exist. Bowker and Star (1999) use the ICD as their running example to show that a classification is built to serve a particular institutional purpose and carries that purpose in its structure. The ICD’s full title designates it a statistical classification. Residual categories of the “not elsewhere classified” form are a designed feature of it rather than a defect, and the decisions embedded in any classification become invisible once the system is in routine use. Scott (1998) describes the same movement at the level of the state: administrative simplification makes a population governable by making it countable, and what the simplification omits ceases to be available to policy. A scoping review of post-Soviet health systems identifies mental health across the region as a blind spot, drawing around three per cent of health budgets, with no tradition of population-wide surveys using international instruments. Residual categories are where things stop being counted.

The gap

Record-linkage studies recover associations already present in existing data, which need only to be extracted. In Kazakhstan the problem arises earlier. No data field records postpartum status, and the national series is compiled from a residual diagnostic code that clinicians are correct not to apply, so the link between a diagnosis and the postpartum period is never recorded at all. No study has examined what detection would require in a health system where the diagnostic category exists but no record of it is produced.

Section 04 · The AI-Assisted Solution, and What It Would Cost

So how do you plan for it?

The proposal

01
The record-linkage layer

Natural language processing would read the free-text clinical notes already held by the Republican Center for Electronic Healthcare, in Russian and in Kazakh, and flag any admission where the notes mention a recent delivery, which would then be confirmed against obstetric records for a birth in the preceding twelve months. What this recovers is the one fact the coding system never captured: that the woman had just given birth. The output is a national and regional estimate of postpartum psychiatric morbidity, built for planners rather than for clinicians.

02
What the estimate makes possible

A reliable estimate of incidence and of its regional distribution is the input that screening-protocol design and the targeting of patronage-nurse visits currently lack. Allocation methods of the kind developed by ARMMAN belong to a later phase, and presuppose that such an estimate already exists. The sequence matters: the second component cannot be built first, and the absence of the first is why Kazakhstan has neither.

Text processing is proposed in preference to record linkage alone because the structured codes lost the association and the clinical free text may not have. A psychiatrist writing an admission note for a woman admitted three weeks after delivery is likely to mention the delivery, since it bears directly on the presentation, even where the coding system provides no field in which that fact can be recorded. The Milan study links structured administrative records to one another, and that method would fail here, because the Kazakhstani structured records contain nothing to link. The CRIS system reads what clinicians wrote rather than what they coded.

Case studies

CRIS · South London and Maudsley NHS Foundation Trust
The methodological precedent

Ayre et al. (2021) built a natural language processing application over de-identified free-text records held in the Clinical Record Interactive Search system, using several layers of linguistic processing based on the spaCy library. The classifier was evaluated on span, status, temporality and polarity against a manually coded reference standard rather than against the diagnostic codes, because the codes were the thing under examination. Linkage with Hospital Episode Statistics allowed a perinatal cohort to be constructed. The wider CRIS NLP service now lists more than eighty such applications in routine deployment, under a standing ethical approval structure.

ARMMAN · with Google Research and IIT Madras
Relevant to the second component only

The ARMMAN programme allocated a limited number of live-service calls across a large maternal health programme using restless multi-armed bandits, drawing on more than 300,000 anonymised call records, and reported reductions in drop-off risk of up to 32 per cent. Its relevance is to the allocation of scarce contact under constraint, not to detection.

ARS Toscana · Milan
The population-scale precedent, and the contrast

The Milan analysis (2026) followed 164,767 mothers who gave birth between 2017 and 2023, applying an identification algorithm across linked administrative healthcare databases to estimate perinatal depression at population scale. It establishes that this kind of estimate can be produced for an entire birth cohort from records collected for other purposes.

The bill

Economic

Borne by — Ministry of Health

The RCEH already operates the infrastructure, so the real costs lie elsewhere: developing clinical natural language processing for two languages with few existing resources, building an annotated reference standard, validating the classifier against it, and maintaining both as documentation practices change. The computational profile is finite — periodic batch processing over a fixed corpus, not continuous inference — though data localisation obliges the processing to be hosted within Kazakhstan, whose grid is predominantly coal-fired.

Substantial, concentrated in bilingual NLP

Political and governance

Borne by — The operator, uninsurably

Law No. 230-VIII, in force since 18 January 2026, establishes a risk-tiered framework. A system of this kind would in all likelihood fall within the high-risk tier, but the obligations attaching to that tier are not concretely specified in the statute, and this underspecification is itself a finding rather than a gap in the research. The law also prohibits the unlawful processing of personal data and the determination of a person’s emotional state without consent, and imposes a mandatory liability insurance requirement whose terms await implementing legislation.

Unquantifiable — the tier’s duties are unwritten

Data ethics

Borne by — Women who never consented

The system would infer, from records women provided in the course of seeking treatment, that they experienced a disorder associated with childbirth, without their knowledge and with no mechanism by which they could decline. The output is statistical, but the pipeline must identify individuals in order to aggregate them, so the de-identification claim applies to the result and not to the process. In oblasts where annual case numbers are small, figures disaggregated by region and year carry a meaningful risk of re-identification.

The binding constraint

The cheapest fix
has no AI in it.

One field on one form · no compute · no governance exposure

The verdict

The concession is larger than the proposal. Adding a field for postpartum status to the existing reporting system would resolve the greater part of this problem going forward, at a fraction of the cost, with none of the governance exposure, and without any artificial intelligence at all.

What remains, then, is justified only for records that already exist and can no longer be coded any other way. A decade of psychiatric admission records exists in which the puerperal association was never coded and cannot now be recovered by any means other than reading what clinicians wrote. No alternative method reaches it, and the estimate it would produce is the precondition for every subsequent decision about screening and service allocation. The answer this analysis supports is a qualified yes, and the qualification carries most of the weight.

Four conditions attach. Outputs must be aggregate only, with regional disaggregation suppressed wherever case numbers are small enough to permit re-identification. No individual-level flag may be returned to a clinician, at any stage or under any subsequent extension of the system. De-identification must occur at the point of extraction rather than at the point of reporting. And the system requires a standing ethical approval structure of the kind CRIS operates, together with a sunset provision, so that a tool built to produce one estimate does not become a permanent register by default.

Any argument that the classifier should also run prospectively would have to establish why it outperforms a checkbox, and this analysis does not establish that.

Verdict

Yes to the statistical layer, under four conditions.

Aggregate-only outputs. No individual flag returned to a clinician. De-identification at extraction, not at reporting. A standing ethics structure and a sunset clause. The concession stands: a postpartum-status field would solve most of this going forward with no AI at all.

AI transparency statement

This project was built in sustained collaboration with an AI assistant, as the course requires. The exported process log accompanies it.

What the AI contributed

It reframed the problem. The project began as an awareness argument — that Kazakhstani women do not know what postpartum depression is — and the registration data contradicted that framing almost immediately. The move from an awareness account to an information-architecture account was proposed by the assistant and adopted after I checked the figures myself. It also identified candidate literature, including Bowker and Star, which became the theoretical anchor of Section 3; it checked arithmetic; and it wrote the HTML and CSS of this site to my direction.

What I rejected

A direct-to-woman chatbot, the obvious proposal, was dropped once the evidence showed no durable effect on wellbeing and the field's most prominent regulated product had been discontinued. A hypothesis that psychiatric registration in Kazakhstan carries legal consequences — which would have strengthened the argument considerably — was dropped after I checked and found no such direct consequences. A UNICEF datapoint showing F53 at 3.4 per cent was dropped as prevalence evidence once I established that it represented a single case in a thirteen-year-old study of a different population.

What the AI got wrong

An early draft of Section 1 claimed that ICD-10 provides no way to attach puerperal status to a psychiatric diagnosis. That is false: the classification provides for dual coding. The error was caught on verification, and the corrected account is stronger, because it locates the failure in Kazakhstan's reporting system rather than in the international standard.

What is not verified by a source

The search results in Figure 5 are composed rather than captured, and are labelled as such on the figure. Figure 3's registered bar is magnified by the factor printed beside it. Every other figure on this site traces to a named source opposite.
